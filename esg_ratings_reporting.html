Why ESG Ratings Don't Measure the Same Thing

Why the same company can look like a leader, a laggard, or something in between depending on which ESG rating system is used.

Shivam AsthanaIndependent research article

Two investors can look at the same company and come away with completely different conclusions about its sustainability performance. One rating system might classify a company as a laggard, another as average, a third as a leader. At first glance that looks like inconsistency, or even a flaw. But the issue is not the company. It is the lens.

ESG ratings are often treated as a single, comparable metric. In reality they are better understood as different analytical models, each designed to answer a different question. That is why MSCI, Sustainalytics, and CDP can score the same company very differently without any of them being wrong.

What ESG ratings are actually measuring

The easiest way to understand ESG ratings is to stop asking which one is correct and instead ask what each rating is trying to measure.

MSCI looks at how well a company manages financially material ESG risks and opportunities, relative to peers.
Sustainalytics focuses on unmanaged ESG risk, how much risk remains after mitigation.
CDP evaluates the quality and completeness of environmental disclosure.

These are not variations of the same score. They are fundamentally different perspectives.

Why the same company gets different scores

ESG ratings are built through a series of choices: objective, scope, data used, measurement approach, and aggregation logic. Small differences at each stage compound into large differences at the end. A widely cited MIT Sloan study, Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon's "Aggregate Confusion: The Divergence of ESG Ratings" (Review of Finance, 2022), decomposed rating divergence across six major agencies and found measurement differences responsible for 56% of the divergence and scope differences for 38%, with weighting differences, the factor most people assume matters most, accounting for only 6%.

A real-world pattern: disclosure vs risk

Companies with strong climate disclosure often score well on CDP without necessarily scoring well on broader ESG ratings. Strong climate disclosure can support a higher CDP score, while broader ESG risks and legacy governance issues weigh on other ratings. The same pattern shows up in sectors like aviation and chemicals, where transparency can improve even while structural emissions exposure remains high.

Why better reporting won't fully solve this

With CSRD in force, there is a growing expectation that standardised reporting will make ESG ratings more comparable. It will help, but only to a point. Standardised disclosures improve data quality and consistency. They do not change what rating providers prioritise or how they interpret the same data.

The real risk is not disagreement between ESG ratings. The real risk is treating them as directly comparable, objective, and decision-ready.

What this means in practice

Match the rating to the objective. Use risk-based ratings for risk decisions and disclosure-based scores for transparency analysis.
Use more than one perspective. Disagreement between ratings is a signal that needs to be understood, not resolved away.
Look beyond the headline score. Underlying drivers matter more than the final number.
Treat ESG ratings like models. They come with assumptions, limitations, and update risk.

ESG ratings do not disagree because one is flawed. They disagree because they are designed to measure different aspects of sustainability. The question is no longer which rating is correct, but what a given rating actually tells you, and whether that is what you need to know.

Prepared as an independent portfolio article on ESG rating divergence. One clarification worth stating explicitly: CDP is primarily a disclosure and environmental questionnaire platform with its own scoring, whereas MSCI and Sustainalytics provide broader ESG or sustainability-risk assessments across environmental, social, and governance dimensions. They are grouped together here because all three illustrate how differently "ESG performance" gets measured, not because they are equivalent products measuring the same thing in different ways.
