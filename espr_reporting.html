ESPR Isn't One Deadline. It's a Rolling Wave, and Steel Is Next.

The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation doesn't hit all at once. It bites one product category at a time, as the Commission adopts each delegated act. Some categories are already live. Iron and steel is next in line.

Shivam AsthanaRegulation Series

Most EU regulations have a single applicability date you can put on a calendar. ESPR doesn't work that way. It's a framework regulation, meaning it sets out a process, the Commission adopts delegated acts category by category, and each one brings its own binding requirements and its own date. Treating ESPR as "not relevant yet" because no single deadline has arrived misses that some categories are already live, and the next one is a core manufacturing input.

What's already binding

Live now

TextilesThe ban on destroying unsold clothing and footwear took effect for large enterprises on 19 July 2026.
Next upIron and steel is prioritised in the ESPR working plan, meaning the Commission has flagged it for an early delegated act. As of publication, that delegated act has not yet been adopted, so no binding iron and steel requirement is in force yet, only a working-plan priority and an indicative 2026 timeline that could move.
LaterElectronics and ICT are confirmed priority categories, but Digital Product Passport obligations for them are phased in 2028 to 2029, with mobile phones and tablets not covered until 2030.

That planned sequencing matters more than it looks, if it holds. Iron and steel arriving before electronics would mean a heavy manufacturing input gets ecodesign and traceability rules attached to it earlier than the consumer product categories most people associate with "ecodesign." A company that assumes ESPR is only a consumer-electronics problem, and therefore someone else's problem, is exactly the company likely to be caught off guard if the current working-plan sequence holds.

The mechanism underneath all of it: the Digital Product Passport

2024ESPR, Regulation (EU) 2024/1781, enters into force, replacing the earlier Ecodesign Directive.
2026Textile destruction ban live. Iron and steel delegated act expected.
2028–29Electronics and ICT Digital Product Passport obligations phase in.
2030Mobile phones and tablets specifically brought into DPP scope.

The Digital Product Passport is the concept that ties every ESPR category together: structured, traceable product data, covering materials, durability, reparability, and recyclability, that has to exist in a standard digital format and follow the product through its life. That's not primarily a compliance requirement, it's a data architecture requirement. A company that only starts thinking about structured product data once its category's delegated act lands is starting from zero on the hardest part.

The DPP isn't a label. It's a data model your product has to carry with it, and someone has to build that model before the deadline, not after.

Cross-reference: the Digital Battery Passport, live from February 2027 under the Batteries Regulation, is explicitly framed as the pilot for this exact concept. Whatever a company learns building that one transfers directly to whatever ESPR category comes for it next.

What this means in practice

Because ESPR arrives one delegated act at a time, the honest operational posture isn't "wait for our category's deadline." It's building the product data architecture, structured, digital, traceable, once, so that whichever category gets a delegated act next is a formatting exercise against data that already exists, rather than a scramble to create it from scratch under a real deadline.

Sources: Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 (ESPR), European Commission ESPR working plan, and current implementation analysis, current as of August 2026.
