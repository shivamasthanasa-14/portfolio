CSDDD Just Got Smaller. For a 50-Person Supplier, That's When It Started Mattering.

The Omnibus package cut the number of companies directly bound by the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive down to a few thousand giants. For everyone below that line, the directive doesn't disappear. It just stops arriving as regulation and starts arriving as a clause in a supplier contract.

Shivam Asthana Manufacturing Regulation Series · 2 of 3

On 24 February 2026, EU member states gave final approval to the "Omnibus I" package, which significantly narrowed the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, CSDDD, sometimes written CS3D. The amendments were published in the Official Journal on 26 February and entered into force on 19 March 2026. Most coverage framed this as deregulation: fewer companies in scope, lighter obligations, a directive that had been "hollowed out."

That's an accurate description of the legal text. It's a misleading description of what it means operationally for a mid-size manufacturer, because CSDDD was never going to reach a 50-person supplier through direct regulation. It was always going to reach them through a contract, and the Omnibus changes actually make that pass-through channel more concentrated, not less relevant.

What the Omnibus actually changed

Key amendments, in force since March 2026

Scope Direct obligations now apply only to EU companies with more than 5,000 employees and over €1.5bn net worldwide turnover. Non-EU companies are scoped by EU-generated turnover above the same threshold; employee count is no longer decisive for them.
Chain depth Due diligence obligations are narrowed from the full value chain to primarily direct (tier-1) business partners. Deeper-tier suppliers only come into scope where there's plausible information indicating a specific risk.
Climate The requirement to adopt a Paris-aligned climate transition plan with Scope 1-3 targets, previously binding on companies above 1,500 employees, has been deleted entirely.
Liability The EU-wide civil liability regime has been removed; liability questions revert to national law.

Timeline

Jul 2024 Original CSDDD (Directive (EU) 2024/1760) enters into force.
Mar 2026 Omnibus amendments (Directive (EU) 2026/470) enter into force, narrowing scope as above.
Jul 2028 Deadline for member state transposition of the amended directive into national law.
Jul 2029 Application begins for companies that meet the revised thresholds.

The Commission's implementation guidelines and model contract clauses are the next concrete step. A consultation on CSDDD implementation guidance ran through mid-August 2026. Those model clauses matter more to a small supplier than the directive text itself, because they're what large customers will actually paste into procurement contracts.

Why this concentrates the pressure on smaller suppliers rather than removing it

Before Omnibus, a manufacturer several tiers removed from an in-scope company might reasonably have been asked, indirectly, about a wide swath of its own supply chain. After Omnibus, the in-scope company's due diligence effort is concentrated almost entirely on its direct business partners, which means if you sell directly to a large in-scope customer, you are now the primary, not secondary, object of their due diligence process.

Before Omnibus

  • Diligence effort spread across the full value chain
  • Deep-tier suppliers occasionally reached indirectly
  • Direct suppliers were one node among many

After Omnibus

  • Diligence effort concentrated on tier-1 partners
  • Deep-tier suppliers reached only if risk-flagged
  • Direct suppliers are now the primary contact point

For a manufacturer that sells directly into a large EU or EU-facing customer's supply chain, that's a meaningful shift. The paperwork doesn't come from Brussels. It comes from the customer's procurement team, in the form of a supplier code of conduct addendum, a due diligence questionnaire, or contractual assurances tied to the OECD's six-step due diligence process, even though the supplier itself has no independent legal obligation under CSDDD at all.

🇩🇪

For manufacturers operating in Germany specifically: the Omnibus text explicitly permits member states to realign existing national due diligence laws, including Germany's Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG), with the new, narrower CSDDD scope. Whether the German legislator actually narrows the LkSG to match is still an open legislative question, which means a German supplier could plausibly face a stricter national standard than the EU floor for some time yet.

The operations angle

The directive shrank. The paperwork didn't move upstream. It moved to whoever signs the supply contract.

The mistake would be filing this under "regulation we don't need to worry about" because the employee and revenue thresholds are so clearly out of reach. The more useful read is that contract and procurement teams, not sustainability teams, are the right owners of this risk for a smaller manufacturer, because the exposure arrives as a clause a customer's legal team wants signed, on a timeline set by that customer's own compliance program, not by the EU's transposition calendar.

Practically, that means treating supplier code-of-conduct requests and due diligence questionnaires from large customers as a standard part of contract review going forward, rather than one-off asks, and being ready to answer the same handful of questions (labor practices, subcontractor oversight, environmental controls in your own operations) repeatedly, from different customers, on different templates, well before 2029.
