The GHG Protocol: The Accounting System Behind Every Corporate Emissions Number

Before a company can report its emissions under CSRD, ISSB, CDP, or any other framework, it needs a methodology for counting them. That methodology is almost always the GHG Protocol. For the first time since 2011, it is being rewritten.

Shivam AsthanaApril 2026

Many major corporate climate-disclosure and target-setting initiatives, CSRD, ISSB, CDP, SBTi, use, reference, or substantially align with the GHG Protocol's emissions-accounting architecture. If you do not understand how emissions are counted, you cannot meaningfully interpret what any of those disclosures actually say.

The three-scope structure

SCOPE 1

Direct emissions

Emissions from sources owned or controlled by the company, combustion in boilers, furnaces, vehicles, and industrial processes.

e.g. fuel burned in a company-owned factory or fleet

SCOPE 2

Purchased energy

Indirect emissions from the generation of electricity, heat, or steam the company buys and consumes.

e.g. emissions from the power grid used to run a data centre

SCOPE 3

Value chain emissions

All other indirect emissions across the upstream and downstream value chain, 15 categories in total.

e.g. supplier production, business travel, product use, end-of-life disposal

This structure is deceptively simple. Scope 3 is often the largest component of a company's value-chain emissions, particularly in sectors such as automotive, consumer goods, food, and technology. A car manufacturer's biggest emission source is not the factory floor, it is the fuel burned by vehicles after they are sold. A company managing only Scope 1 and 2 may be accounting for a small fraction of its true footprint, the exact share varies significantly by sector and business model.

Why it became the global standard

Developed jointly by the World Resources Institute and the World Business Council for Sustainable Development, first published 2004, updated with the Scope 3 Standard in 2011. It achieved dominance not through regulation but adoption: thousands of companies converged on the same framework because the alternative, fragmented, incompatible methodologies, made comparison impossible. CSRD and ESRS require GHG Protocol-aligned accounting. ISSB's IFRS S2 is built on the same scope structure. CDP scores companies using it. SBTi sets targets in terms of it.

The standards are now being rewritten

The Corporate Standard has not been updated since 2004. The Scope 3 Standard dates from 2011. In 2025, the GHG Protocol launched a formal revision process across all three standards simultaneously. The timeline below reflects the GHG Protocol's published Standard Development Plan as of late 2025 and is indicative, the process is ongoing and dates may shift as the working groups progress.

2025Public consultations opened on Scope 2 and consequential accounting methods. Working groups formed for Corporate Standard and Scope 3.
2026Draft revised standards, Corporate, Scope 2, and Scope 3, scheduled for public consultation mid-year.
2027Final revised standards published. Current standards remain in effect until transition timelines are confirmed.
2028New guidance on Actions and Market Instruments, covering carbon credits, RECs, and decarbonisation project accounting.

What's actually under revision

Scope 2, hourly matching: the current method allows annual renewable energy certificates. The revision proposes hourly matching by location, closing a loophole letting companies claim clean energy that doesn't correspond to actual consumption timing.
Scope 3, data quality: spend-based estimation is widely used but imprecise. The revision pushes toward activity-based and supplier-specific data, more accurate but harder to obtain.
Alignment with ISSB and CSRD: explicitly harmonising definitions and boundaries with IFRS S2 and ESRS to reduce the interpretive gap.
Market instruments: clearer rules on carbon credits in inventory accounting, addressing the debate over genuine reductions versus offset claims.

Companies that have already invested in supplier-level data and granular energy tracking will find the revision represents validation, not disruption.

What this means for practitioners

Current standards stay in effect until revised versions are published and adopted by disclosure programs. Nothing changes immediately. But the direction is clear: less interpretive flexibility, stronger data requirements, tighter alignment with mandatory disclosure. Understanding the existing framework well enough to identify where the revisions will bite, Scope 2 matching, Scope 3 data quality, market instrument rules, is the kind of analytical work that adds genuine value ahead of a regulatory shift.
