EUDR Has Been Delayed Twice. December 2026 Might Actually Stick.

Originally due at the end of 2024, then pushed to the end of 2025, the Deforestation Regulation now applies to large and medium operators from 30 December 2026. Here's why this delay looks different from the last two, and where the real exposure sits even for companies that don't touch a rainforest.

Shivam AsthanaRegulation Series

The EU Deforestation Regulation has a genuine credibility problem, not with its substance, but with its timeline. Two postponements in two years teaches companies to treat every new date as provisional. That instinct is understandable and, this time, probably wrong.

What actually changed in May 2026

The Commission's simplification package, published in May 2026, is widely reported as the reason to take the December 2026 date seriously. Current analysis describes it as ruling out a further postponement, cutting compliance costs by an estimated 75 percent, and keeping the core due diligence obligations intact, though the precise legal text is worth checking directly rather than relying on secondary summaries for a compliance decision. Directionally, a regulator publishing a package built around a fixed date is a different signal than one still leaving room for delay.

The obvious exposure is a cocoa importer. The one companies actually miss is checking whether their own tyres count.

Where the exposure hides

The commodity list looks narrow, seven items, until you trace what's actually derived from them. Natural rubber, used in tyres, is one of the seven, which means automotive and logistics operations should at minimum check whether their tyre supply chain falls under the regulation's product classification, rather than assuming it doesn't just because they don't import raw rubber. Whether a specific tyre or downstream use is actually in scope depends on how that product is classified under the regulation, not on the commodity list alone. Paper and cardboard packaging made from wood fibre is a similar case worth checking: the same physical box can potentially be regulated under EUDR as a wood-derived product and under PPWR as packaging, for two different reasons, though again the actual classification needs verifying rather than assuming.

Cross-reference: that packaging overlap is covered in more detail in the PPWR article. If your company handles cardboard packaging at any volume, both regulations apply to the same material at once.

What this means in practice

Given the pattern of prior delays, a company that waited for certainty before starting geolocation data collection has, twice now, been rewarded for waiting. The May 2026 package is the signal that this pattern is ending. For companies with real exposure through the seven named commodities or their derivatives, treating December 2026 as fixed and starting supplier geolocation data collection now is the defensible position, even though a third delay can never be ruled out completely.
