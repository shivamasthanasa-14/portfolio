CSRD & ESRS: What the Framework Actually Requires, and What Just Changed

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive is the most ambitious sustainability disclosure law the EU has ever passed. After a year of political pressure, it looks significantly different from what companies were originally preparing for.

Shivam AsthanaApril 2026

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive replaced the Non-Financial Reporting Directive and massively expanded who had to report, and what they had to say. The key architectural idea is double materiality: companies disclose both how sustainability risks affect their business and how their business affects people and the environment. The technical substance is defined by the European Sustainability Reporting Standards, ESRS, drafted by EFRAG, covering environmental, social, and governance topics with significant quantitative requirements throughout.

Who was originally in scope

CSRD was designed to roll out in waves, progressively pulling in more companies over time.

WaveCompaniesOriginal timeline
Wave 1~1,000Large public-interest entities, 500+ employees, reporting from 2025 for FY2024
Wave 2~15,000Other large EU undertakings, originally due 2026 for FY2025
Wave 3~3,500Listed SMEs, originally due 2027 for FY2026

With non-EU companies carrying significant EU revenue added as Wave 4, total scope reached around 50,000 entities globally. That was always politically ambitious, and it did not survive contact with the 2025 competitiveness debate.

The Omnibus intervention

The Commission's original simplification proposal was published in February 2025. After a year of negotiation, the final Directive (EU) 2026/470 was published in the Official Journal on 26 February 2026 and entered into force on 19 March 2026, amending CSRD, the EU Taxonomy, and CSDDD simultaneously. Framed as simplification, in practice it recalibrated how far the EU was willing to push mandatory sustainability obligations in the near term.

Scope reduction: mandatory reporting now generally applies where an entity exceeds both 1,000 employees on average and €450 million in net annual turnover, subject to detailed rules for different entity types and non-EU groups. Estimates of the resulting population vary, but commonly cited figures put in-scope EU companies at roughly 5,000, down from around 50,000 under the original thresholds.
Stop-the-Clock: Wave 2 and Wave 3 reporting delayed by two years. Companies that would have reported in 2026 now do not report until 2028.
ESRS simplification: EFRAG's revised standards, submitted to the Commission in December 2025, materially reduced the number and complexity of required datapoints. The exact reduction depends on whether the comparison is made against the original standards or the final delegated act, which was still in its adoption process as of publication here. Sector-specific standards were dropped entirely.
Assurance: limited assurance remains the relevant initial requirement. The revised framework removes or changes the previous pathway toward reasonable assurance, subject to the final legal text and applicable assurance standards, which the Commission is required to adopt by 1 October 2026.
Value chain cap: the revised CSRD limits the sustainability information reporting companies may require from value-chain undertakings with fewer than 1,000 employees, generally aligning such requests with the voluntary reporting standard and proportionality principles, rather than an outright prohibition.
Double materiality: the principle survives, but the assessment process is intended to be more proportionate. Prescriptive requirements around the process are reduced, and companies may rely on reasonable and supportable information without undue cost or effort, though they should still be able to demonstrate a defensible assessment.

Omnibus I narrowed the regulation. It did not end it. For roughly 5,000 companies, mandatory reporting under ESRS remains a legal requirement from 2027 onward.

Why this matters beyond compliance

There is a temptation to treat CSRD as a compliance exercise to be managed and minimised. That misses the strategic dimension. The double materiality lens, even simplified, forces companies to map where sustainability intersects with business risk and opportunity in both directions. Companies that use CSRD as an analytical tool, not a reporting burden, leave the process with a sharper understanding of where they are exposed and where they can build advantage.

Sources: Directive (EU) 2026/470 (Omnibus I), EFRAG technical advice December 2025, and analysis from Deloitte, PwC, and Morrison Foerster.
