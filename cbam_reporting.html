CBAM Is Already Live. The Certificates Aren't the Hard Part.

Most coverage of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism talks about certificate purchases starting in 2027. The obligations that actually bite, installation-level emissions data, importer registration, quarterly reporting, are live now, and they are the harder problem.

Shivam AsthanaRegulation Series

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive regime on 1 January 2026, three years after the transitional reporting phase began in October 2023. The financial mechanism, buying certificates to cover the embedded carbon in imported goods, doesn't start until importers purchase their first certificates in 2027. But treating 2027 as the real deadline misses where the actual work has to happen.

What's live right now

Binding since 1 January 2026

StatusImporters of covered goods above the 50 tonne threshold generally need Authorised CBAM Declarant status, with specific transitional and procedural rules governing exactly when that status is required and how it's obtained.
ScopeSix named sectors only: iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen, and electricity. Nothing else is covered under the current text.
ReportingEmissions must be reported at the individual production installation level, not as a company-wide or product-category average.
Data gapWithout verified supplier-specific data, conservative default values apply automatically, and they push the effective cost higher than real supplier data would.

That last point is where most of the practical difficulty sits. An importer with strong supplier relationships and real installation-level emissions data pays based on that data. An importer without it pays the default, which is deliberately set high enough to create an incentive to go get the real numbers. Chasing that data down before certificate purchases start in 2027 is the actual project, not a compliance afterthought.

Where the scope is heading

Oct 2023Transitional reporting phase begins. No financial obligation yet, quarterly reports only.
Jan 2026Definitive regime begins. Authorised Declarant status required, installation-level reporting live.
Sep 2027First certificate purchases required, covering the prior year's imports.
Jan 2028Proposed downstream expansion to roughly 180 additional product categories, per the Commission's December 2025 proposal, not yet adopted by Council and Parliament.

The 2028 expansion proposal, published by the Commission in December 2025, is worth watching, but it is currently a proposal, not an adopted rule, and it has not been agreed by the Council and Parliament. If adopted as proposed, it would pull in downstream products with meaningful embedded steel or aluminium content, machinery, auto parts, and household goods have been cited as likely categories in the Commission's own impact assessment, which references embedded steel and aluminium averaging around 79 percent of value for some of those goods. Whether the final text matches the proposal, and whether the 2028 date holds, remains genuinely open. A company that has never thought about CBAM because it doesn't produce raw steel or cement is worth watching this proposal rather than assuming it's settled either way.

The certificate is the bill. The installation-level data is the actual homework, and it's due first.

The operational split

CBAM doesn't have one natural owner inside a company, and that's part of what makes it hard to manage well. Trade and customs teams own Authorised Declarant status and the certificate purchase process itself. Procurement owns the harder, longer job: getting verified installation-level emissions data out of suppliers who may never have been asked for it before. Finance owns forecasting certificate costs against a price that moves with the EU ETS market, and increasingly, disclosing that exposure as a financial risk.

Cross-reference: that last point, disclosing CBAM cost exposure as a financial risk, may be relevant to ESRS E1-9's anticipated financial effects disclosure, subject to the company's own materiality assessment and the final applicable ESRS wording. See the audit control matrix for how that kind of control gets documented in practice.

What this means in practice

A company covered by CBAM's current six sectors, or watching the 2028 proposal because it might be covered if that expansion is adopted, generally gains more from starting supplier data collection now than from waiting for certificate purchases to force the issue. The default values are set conservatively high, which functions as a real incentive to obtain supplier-specific data, whether or not that was the regulation's explicit design intent. The practical way to avoid paying the default is the same data infrastructure work that shows up everywhere else in this track, structured collection, a named owner, and evidence that survives scrutiny.

Sources: Regulation (EU) 2023/956 (CBAM), European Commission implementation guidance, the Commission's December 2025 downstream expansion proposal, and analysis from DEHSt, EY, and ICAP, current as of August 2026.
