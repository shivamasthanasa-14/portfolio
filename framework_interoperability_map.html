Collect Once, Report Many Times

A large manufacturer answers to ESRS because EU law requires it, to GRI and increasingly ISSB because global stakeholders expect them, and often to rating platforms like CDP or EcoVadis because a customer or investor asked for them directly. Collect the same numbers separately for each and costs balloon, timelines slip, and the same fact risks being reported two different ways. This shows where one number can serve several audiences, and where the evidence for that overlap is actually documented versus just claimed.

Two Different Categories, Not One List

Rulebooks, and platforms companies get asked to join

ESRS, GRI, and ISSB (IFRS S1/S2) are standards. Each is written by an official standard-setting body and defines exactly what a company must disclose. ESRS is mandatory under EU law for companies in CSRD scope. GRI is a voluntary global standard unless a jurisdiction makes it mandatory. ISSB is the same idea from a different standard-setter, increasingly being adopted into national law elsewhere. These three bodies have published joint documents with each other stating how their rules correspond.

CDP and EcoVadis are a different category: rating platforms. Neither is a standard-setter. Both run a questionnaire and produce a score, and companies respond because an investor or customer asked for it, not because a law requires it. They belong together on this page as two examples of the same kind of thing, not as one primary case and one afterthought.

The two categories also differ in how solid the evidence is. The three standards have jointly published, standard-setter-endorsed mapping documents. For the rating platforms, that evidence is thinner, and not equally thin for both, as shown below.

The Rating Platforms

CDP and EcoVadis, side by side, not ranked

CDP

Scored climate, water, and forests questionnaire

JOINTLY PUBLISHED MAPPING EXISTS

CDP and EFRAG published a formal Correspondence Mapping between the CDP question bank and ESRS E1 datapoints in March 2025. Current figures put the overlap at roughly 75 percent of ESRS E1's disclosure requirements.

That mapping is built on the 2024 CDP questionnaire against the pre-Omnibus ESRS E1. CDP has stated it will publish an updated mapping once the revised, simplified ESRS is formally in force.

EcoVadis

Scored sustainable procurement rating

VENDOR-STATED ALIGNMENT, NO JOINT INDEX

EcoVadis states that its methodology aligns with ESRS and offers a dedicated CSRD data collection tool for supplier reporting. No jointly published, standard-setter-endorsed mapping document exists for this pairing, unlike CDP's, and no independently verified overlap percentage is available.

Treat EcoVadis's alignment claim as a starting point for a company's own gap analysis, not as documented fact the way the CDP figure above can be treated.

Common Architecture

The framework that no longer exists is still the skeleton

TCFD, the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures, was formally disbanded in 2023, with its responsibilities absorbed into the IFRS Foundation from 2024. Its four-pillar structure strongly influenced the architecture of IFRS S2 and remains visible in climate-disclosure questionnaires such as CDP's, though the standards and questionnaires are not identical to it. A framework that no longer exists is still shaping how the standards and the rating platforms above are organized today.

Why Full Overlap Never Happens

The three reasons standards still diverge

Granularity

Same topic, different level of detail or a different data type requested for what is nominally the same disclosure.

Scope

One standard covers ground the other simply does not touch. Certain GRI governance topics, tax transparency among them, fall outside the ESRS materiality list entirely.

Definition

Both standards ask about the same underlying concept, worded differently enough that a direct copy-paste answer would not actually satisfy either one.

Collecting the data once is the goal. Formatting it several different ways is the actual job.

The three standards were not built independently of each other, EFRAG's own interoperability workstream exists specifically to avoid double reporting. The two rating platforms are a looser fit, useful, sometimes commercially unavoidable, but resting on thinner and unequal evidence. A well-designed data architecture treats those two facts differently: build the core collection process around whichever standard is legally binding, reuse it wherever a joint mapping exists, and treat the rest as a gap analysis to run periodically, not as data collection to duplicate from scratch.
